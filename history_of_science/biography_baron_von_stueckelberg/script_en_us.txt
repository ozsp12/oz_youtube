This is Ernst Stueckelberg. He was a physicist, a mathematician, and also a baron. Yes, a real baron. Even so, almost no one knows his name. But they should. He had brilliant ideas that were either never published or appeared in low-impact journals.

In 1935, Stueckelberg, a professor in Switzerland, had an incredible idea: perhaps the forces inside an atomic nucleus occurred through the exchange of small particles, called bosons. Today, that idea is a fundamental concept in physics. But at the time, no one took it seriously.

One of the most respected physicists of that era, Wolfgang Pauli, said the idea was ridiculous. Discouraged, Stueckelberg decided not to publish it. Not long after, another physicist, Hideki Yukawa, presented the same idea and won the Nobel Prize for it.

Even so, Stueckelberg kept working. In the 1940s, he began tackling a major problem: quantum physics calculations were giving infinite results. He proposed a new approach to address this issue. Today, we refer to it as renormalization, one of the foundational concepts in modern physics.

But when he submitted his paper to the leading scientific journal of the time, it was rejected. The editors said it wasn’t really a paper,  just a rough outline of an idea. Meanwhile, other physicists, Richard Feynman, Julian Schwinger, and Shin'ichiro Tomonaga, published their own versions and won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965.

In 1941, Stueckelberg also wrote about another bold idea: describing antiparticles as regular particles moving backward in time. Years later, that very concept became the basis for Feynman's famous diagrams.

Once again, Stueckelberg's paper was rejected. He tried to improve it, but Feynman published first and got the credit.

Stueckelberg still contributed other important ideas. He developed a method for giving mass to certain particles, similar to what the Higgs boson does today. And he helped scientists understand how particles behave in theories that combine quantum mechanics and relativity.

Murray Gell-Mann, Nobel laureate in physics in 1969 and a close friend of Feynman's, once wrote a letter pretending to be Stueckelberg. Addressed to Feynman, it was short and to the point: “Give me back my manuscripts.”. The running joke between them was that Feynman had read Stueckelberg’s papers and published the ideas as if they were his own.

Ernst Stueckelberg died in 1984, almost completely unknown. But today, scientists and historians are rediscovering his work, because science isn’t always fair; however, the truth eventually finds its way.
